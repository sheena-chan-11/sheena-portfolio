# Women’s Wilderness case-study draft: review

Reviewed October 31, 2026. This review concerns the supplied draft, not the underlying research recordings or report. No portfolio pages were changed.

## Overall assessment

The draft has a credible foundation: a real nonprofit client, a bounded one-month engagement, a five-person research team, six participants, concrete usability concerns, and stakeholder feedback. The largest opportunity is to replace a chronology of activities with an evidence-to-decision story that makes your individual contribution visible.

## Highest-priority revisions

1. **Lead with the research problem, not rhetorical questions.** Explain the organization briefly, then frame the investigation around finding suitable programs and understanding registration availability. Do not imply this study established demographic participation gaps or economic barriers if those questions were not investigated.
2. **Separate organizational audience from study sample.** The organization’s current mission includes girls, women, and nonbinary people of all ages and backgrounds. If ages 18–25 describes recruitment, label it as the study scope, not general program eligibility.
3. **Show your role.** State the work you personally performed, distinguishing it from the team’s work. Add project dates, duration, team, methods, and deliverables in a compact overview. Use placeholders until these details are verified.
4. **Name methods accurately.** Interviews capture reported experiences and attitudes; observing participants complete tasks is usability testing. If sessions combined both, describe that combination. Researcher-led walkthroughs are not user testing. The reflection should distinguish initial testing of the existing site from the absence of follow-up testing of recommendations.
5. **Connect evidence to recommendations.** For each finding, provide a task/context, observation or authentic anonymized quote, interpretation, proposed change, and validation plan. Include participant counts only when the notes support them; do not invent quotes, percentages, or outcomes.
6. **Fix the registration-state logic.** Distinguish sold-out upcoming events, deadlines that have passed, and completed events. A cancellation waitlist may fit the first, not all three. Consider next-date notifications, alternatives, and clearer status labels for the others. Verify what the competitor’s waitlist actually does.
7. **Calibrate impact.** The client’s quote supports perceived usefulness and stakeholder confidence. It does not establish improved findability, completed registrations, or implementation. Explicitly identify what was delivered, adopted, implemented, and measured—or unknown.

## Section-specific suggestions

### Background and goals

Suggested title: **Helping visitors find suitable outdoor programs at Women’s Wilderness**.

Suggested opening:

> During a one-month pro-bono engagement, our five-person UX research team investigated how prospective participants discovered programs and navigated registration on the Women’s Wilderness website. We compared a peer organization’s website and conducted research with six potential participants, identifying opportunities to clarify program labels, improve filter discoverability, and communicate registration availability.

Use the opening only if it accurately reflects the work. Add your individual responsibilities directly below it.

Consider one main question: **How might we help prospective participants find suitable outdoor programs and understand how to register?** Treat the comparison as a supporting research question, not the same level as the user problem.

### Desk research / initial review

Rename this section **Initial website review** unless it also includes documentary or secondary research. Distinguish researcher impressions from participant findings. Claims that mountain footage creates familiarity or photography makes everyone feel welcome need attribution or more cautious phrasing.

### Competitive analysis

Explain why Backcountry Beauties was relevant, the shared task used to compare sites, and what you learned beyond copying features. Preserve the strength you identified: Women’s Wilderness already surfaced date, price, and location in previews. Consider keeping affinity-program distinctions while clarifying their labels, rather than flattening meaningful categories.

### Participants, methods, and synthesis

Add recruitment criteria, relevant participant characteristics, prior familiarity, session format, tasks, and actual duration if documented. Explain how observations became codes and themes, how the team reconciled interpretations, and how recommendations were prioritized. Color coding alone does not explain the analytical reasoning.

### Findings

Use three finding blocks:

- **Unclear program labels made options difficult to distinguish.** Show exact labels, where they differed, and the observed or reported consequence.
- **The program finder was difficult to discover.** Separate discoverability from the separate hypothesis that combined filters would improve narrowing. Clarify whether the request concerns combining activity/age/type or selecting multiple values within one category.
- **Registration status left visitors without a clear next step.** Identify the actual event state before recommending a waitlist or notification.

Use a consistent structure: **evidence → interpretation → recommendation → next test**. State whether any mockup is a proposal, an implemented change, or a tested design.

### Outcomes and reflection

Retitle the outcome section **Deliverables and client response** if no downstream outcomes were measured. Shorten the testimonial using an exact excerpt from the supplied quote and verify attribution and permission.

Frame reflection as a future action plus its reason. Additional competitors should answer a specific comparison gap. Missing parents matters if youth registration was within scope; otherwise it is a separate future audience, not necessarily a flaw in an adult-focused study. Propose follow-up tasks and metrics rather than claiming untested recommendations worked.

## Recommended structure

1. Summary, role, timeframe, team, and research status
2. Client context and research question
3. Participants and methods
4. Competitive comparison
5. Three evidence-backed findings and recommendations
6. Deliverables, stakeholder response, and outcome limits
7. Reflection and next validation steps

Keep section jump links optional: readers should understand the narrative without clicking. Prefer annotated static comparisons for labels and previews, a short recording for a genuinely important interaction, and captions identifying research date and whether a view is original or proposed.

## Current-site verification caveats

The current Women’s Wilderness site describes an all-ages audience and exposes a program finder on its general Programs page. Date your historical observations and use the screenshots captured during the original research; do not treat today’s site as proof of what existed during the study or as evidence your recommendations were implemented.

Backcountry Beauties currently describes some trip waitlists as early-access/planning participation. That is not necessarily the same as a cancellation queue for sold-out events. Verify the historical flow before describing it as one.
